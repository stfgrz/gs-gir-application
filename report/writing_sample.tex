\section*{Who Has Been Most Exposed}
The usual measure of exposure to Treasuries is a beta: how many basis points a country's 10-year yield moves for every 1bp move in the US 10-year. Since 2000, Canada, Australia and New Zealand have imported 70--80\% of US moves, Germany about 60\%, Switzerland under 40\% and Japan under a fifth.

An average beta, however, hides what an investor cares about: does a market share the rallies as well as the sell-offs? I therefore estimate separate betas for months when US yields rose and fell, a bond-market version of downside beta (Exhibit~\ref{ex:betas}). While Canada, Norway and Sweden move with Treasuries both ways, the euro periphery, Central Europe and South Africa have imported 75--100\% of US sell-offs but at most 30\% of rallies. Since 2013 the UK and France, symmetric before, have joined them: gilts now import sell-offs one-for-one (1.02) but less than half of rallies (0.48).

\section*{Why: Foreign Money and Risk Premia}
Two arguments have usually explained this. Markets that rely on foreign money lose it when US yields rise, and the `Fragile Five' of 2013 were defined by their current-account deficits. And US yields tend to fall when risk appetite does, which is when sovereign, currency and liquidity premia widen in these markets and offset the rally. The data bear this out: allowing for the VIX roughly halves the post-2013 gap in South Africa, Hungary and Italy, and the gap is widest in stress episodes such as the euro crisis or March 2020.

\section*{From Current Accounts to Budgets}
The risk-premium argument still holds, but the external one has given way to a fiscal one (Exhibit~\ref{ex:fault}). The 2000--12 asymmetry lined up with current-account deficits. Since 2013 it lines up with budget deficits instead, with each point of deficit adding about 0.06 to the gap, and the current account no longer matters: Spain and Italy swung into current-account surplus, yet became more one-way. That fits the shifts in Exhibit~\ref{ex:betas}: the UK has run the largest average deficit in my G10 sample since 2013, France is in yet another budget fight, and Poland and Hungary, on course for deficits above 7\% of GDP, have more than doubled their sell-off betas. Japan and India are the exceptions: large deficits, but domestic investor bases that do not run.

\section*{This Year's Test}
US 10-year yields have risen 113bp since late February, to 5.10\%, almost all through real yields, while breakevens have not budged even with Brent near \$100 (Exhibit~\ref{ex:test}a). Real-yield moves are the ones that spill over: since 2003, a 1bp rise in US real yields has lifted Italian yields by 0.6bp, a rise in breakevens by only 0.2bp.

Out of sample, the old map held: when I apply each market's pre-2026 betas to this year's monthly US moves, every market but Japan lands within the range its usual noise implies (Exhibit~\ref{ex:test}b). JGB yields rose 70bp more than implied as the BoJ hiked to 1.25\% and investors balked at the government's fiscal plans. France came closest: +52bp, against a range of $\pm$54bp.

\section*{What It Means for Investors}
First, GS's outlook has the US 10-year at 4.6\% by end-2027, and on post-2013 betas a steady 50bp rally would take Canadian yields down 43bp, gilts 24bp and BTPs less than 10bp, widening BTP–Bund by about 15bp. The path matters too: with one 50bp sell-off on the way, BTPs would end 34bp higher, although over a year local news dominates, with 90\% ranges from $\pm$58bp for Canada to $\pm$220bp for Hungary. Second, periphery carry is short Treasury volatility: since 2000, BTP– and Bono–Bund spreads have widened about 0.1bp per bp when Treasuries sold off and 0.3--0.4bp when they rallied. The main risk is a US-specific sell-off that weakens the dollar, as in April 2025, when Treasuries sold off while Bunds held firm.
